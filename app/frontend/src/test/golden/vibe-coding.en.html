Vibe Coding

Welcome to Vibe Coding: The easiest way to generate SLOP.

Marvin Jakob Updated: 16 November 2026 · approx. 10 min read

You describe what you want and AI writes the code. It is fun. Brilliant. You watch entire worlds emerge before your eyes, looking roughly like what you imagined. If they do not, you follow up with another deeply considered, precise requirement: “Make it look a bit more modern somehow.” And just like that, you have burned through another batch of tokens and helped add something utterly mediocre to the great pile of pointless things.

Suddenly, almost no time separates an idea from something you can show. A prototype is allowed to be improvised. For businesses, that is extremely tempting. It has rounded corners, a dark mode and a home page promising “Your productivity. Reimagined.” We discover what happens next when somebody wants to put it into production. An application can look impressive and still fall apart the first time someone seriously tries to use it.

While we are still wondering whether the first solution made sense, AI has already implemented three more. Code is suddenly available in abundance. Responsibility for the result still rests with us. So we need to judge what is being created. But can we still do that when AI writes new features faster than we understand the existing ones? Anyone determined to personally inspect every generated line has just created a very exhausting job as a human brake.

Humans should review ideas, not code.

These five engineering perspectives help us incorporate that responsibility into the development process.

01

Prompt Engineering

Make the assignment clear.

Writing a good prompt means formulating a request so that AI understands as clearly as possible what it should do. That includes the desired result, the target audience, important constraints and the format. Explaining why also helps AI choose between several possible solutions.

A good prompt improves the starting conditions but does not guarantee a correct answer. Nevertheless, the principle “Shit in → Shit out” remains fundamentally true.

02

Context Engineering

AI unfortunately missed the crucial meeting.

Context Engineering is about providing the information relevant to each task and keeping it up to date throughout the work: appropriate code excerpts, interfaces, project rules, business terminology and earlier decisions.

What matters is that AI finds the right knowledge at the right time and can recognise contradictory or outdated information. Otherwise it implements an exceptionally elegant solution for a company that has not existed since the last restructuring.

03

Harness Engineering

“Work-life balance” for AI.

AI now knows its assignment and our project. To make reliable progress across several steps, it needs a technical working environment. We equip our agent with tools to use interfaces and perform actions. Skilled administrators can add rules specific to an organisation. Among other things, this lets them manage approvals and access permissions.

In Harness Engineering we control the capabilities our agent receives to complete our task.

04

Specification Engineering

“Are we there yet?”

For our agent to judge when it is “finished”, it needs a yardstick. We should first become clear ourselves about what the desired result actually is. Then we turn our ideas into clear, verifiable and maintained descriptions of the desired behaviour. “Or how about the same thing in green?” Design and look and feel belong here too.

And when requirements change, descriptions and checks must change with them. Otherwise we automate the wishes of the Thursday before last with impressive consistency.

05

Product Engineering

Does anyone still need this form, or can it go?

We talk to people, observe their workflows, test assumptions with small experiments and see whether the finished solution actually saves time or reduces errors. Product Engineering connects an understanding of user problems with design, technical implementation and learning during operation.

AI can accelerate these experiments. We still have to clarify which problems to solve and how to recognise success. Otherwise we create extra work faster and give it a very tasteful user interface.

Conclusion

AI has increased the pace. Now raise the ambition.

AI has increased the pace. Now would be a good time to raise our ambitions too. The five engineering perspectives help us use the speed we have gained wisely. They give our work enough reliability to make us more adventurous. This creates room to be curious, question familiar habits and pursue ideas that do not yet come with a guarantee of success. Creativity rarely follows the project plan, after all. It would be a shame if AI gave us time for everything except an original thought.

That is precisely where a great opportunity lies: we can experiment more, design more boldly and find out what actually helps people move forward. This requires our imagination, our judgement and occasionally the charming audacity to challenge what used to be taken for granted. If we can already develop software at a previously unknown pace, we might as well come up with something interesting.
